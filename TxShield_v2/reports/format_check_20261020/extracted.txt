TxShield: Transaction-level RAG-enabled DeFi

Wei Ma†, Shiya Li†, Jiayi Xu, Ye Liu*, Junjie Shi, Cong Wu, Jing Chen, Lingxiao Jiang, and Zijian Zhang
Abstract—Decentralized Finance (DeFi) protocols have become
prime targets for sophisticated attacks, with losses exceeding
$3.4 billion in 2025. However, existing detection approaches face
fundamental limitations. Traditional rule-based detection tools
are limited in their ability to capture diverse attack behaviors
at runtime, while advanced large language models (LLMs) ex-
hibit limitations due to insufficient task-specific knowledge and
hallucinations. To this end, we observe that DeFi attacks, despite
diverse implementations, exhibit recurring behavioral patterns
at the semantic level, suggesting that their apparent diversity
masks a small number of underlying semantic structures. In
this paper, we present TXSHIELD, a Retrieval-Augmented Gen-
eration framework for transaction-level DeFi attack detection.
TXSHIELDconstructs a knowledge base of historical attacks with
LLM-generated behavioral summaries, retrieves historical cases
and applies similarity filtering to select relevant historical context,
and selects prompts according to the available context to guide
LLM reasoning by analogy with retrieved patterns. XGBoost
estimates transaction risk to control the use of retrieved cases
and provides an auxiliary probability hint for LLM reasoning.
The LLM produces the final classification.
On an evaluation set constructed from 364 DeFiHackLabs at-
tack transactions and 364 benign transactions, TXSHIELD achieves
92.93% F1 and 97.87% precision. Compared with direct XGBoost
classification, it improves F1 by 6.45 percentage points and reduces
the number of false-positive transactions from 60 to 7.

I. INTRODUCTION
Decentralized Finance (DeFi) has emerged as a major applica-
tion of blockchain technology, enabling permissionless financial
services through smart contracts. By late 2025, the total value
locked (TVL) in DeFi protocols had exceeded $230 billion,
reflecting the substantial financial value managed by DeFi
systems [1]. This rapid growth has attracted a large user base,
with tens of millions of users interacting with DeFi protocols
worldwide. The increasing scale of DeFi has been accompanied
by significant security challenges. Industry analyses report that

smart-contract vulnerabilities, such as reentrancy as seen

cannot be patched retroactively, making effective testing and
continuous post-deployment monitoring critical for mitigating
security threats in DeFi systems.
Existing approaches to smart contract security can be broadly
categorized into four paradigms, each with inherent limitations.
Static analysis tools such as Slither [5] and Mythril [6] use
pattern matching and symbolic execution to identify known
vulnerability patterns. While effective at detecting classic bugs
such as reentrancy, these tools suffer from path explosion
in complex contracts and often struggle to capture runtime
behaviors or cross-protocol interactions arising from DeFi
composability [7]. Formal verification frameworks like Certora
Prover [8] and KEVM [9] provide mathematical guarantees but
require substantial manual effort to specify invariants and often
struggle to model the dynamic, multi-protocol nature of DeFi.
Rule-based dynamic detection tools such as DeFiRanger [10],
DeFiTainter [11], and DeFort [12] analyze transaction traces to
detect price-manipulation attacks. However, these approaches
rely on predefined mathematical models of specific pricing
mechanisms (e.g., constant-product AMMs), making them less
effective for custom protocols with non-standard price models;
DeFiScope reports that such custom price models account for
44.2% of 95 real-world price manipulation attacks collected
over the past three years [13]. LLM-based approaches represent
a recent paradigm shift. TrxGNNBERT [14] leverages a graph-
transformer language model to learn trace-level representations
for DeFi fraud detection, while GPTScan [15] combines
GPT with static analysis for logic-vulnerability detection
and iAudit [16] employs fine-tuned models with multi-agent
debate for source-code auditing. However, these methods
either focus primarily on pre-deployment source-code analysis
and cannot detect runtime attacks, or incur high end-to-end
overhead for trace construction and model inference, limiting
practicality for real-time monitoring. LLM hallucinations can
lead to plausible but incorrect vulnerability reports. Moreover,
parametric knowledge may lack the transaction-specific context
needed to distinguish an attack from benign behavior.
Our key insight is that DeFi attacks, despite their diverse
manifestations, often exhibit recurring behavioral patterns at
the semantic level. For example, a flash-loan-based price

manipulation attack can follow a “borrow, manipulate, and
profit” sequence: obtaining large capital via flash loans,
executing trades that artificially inflate or deflate token prices,
and extracting profit before repaying the loan, all within a single
atomic transaction. This observation suggests that historical
attack cases, annotated with behavioral semantics, can serve as a
valuable knowledge source to guide the detection of new attacks.
To empirically ground this insight, we analyze approximately
200 real-world DeFi attack incidents from DeFiHackLabs [17].
Despite their apparent diversity, these attacks consistently fall
into eight major semantic categories, with Business Logic
Flaws, Price Manipulation, and Reentrancy-based call attacks
dominating the incident landscape. These results provide
quantitative evidence that attack patterns are reusable across
different protocols.
However, realizing this insight poses three technical chal-
lenges: (C1) Semantic Gap: Raw execution traces consist of
low-level EVM operations and function calls that are difficult to
interpret. How can we bridge the gap between low-level traces
and high-level attack semantics? (C2) Retrieval Precision:
Na¨ıve similarity search may retrieve irrelevant or misleading
historical cases, introducing noise that can degrade detection
accuracy. How can we ensure that retrieved cases are truly
relevant to the query transaction? (C3) Reasoning Reliability:
Even with relevant context, LLMs may hallucinate or fail to
correctly apply retrieved knowledge. How can we guide the
LLM to make reliable attack-or-benign decisions?
To address these challenges, we propose TXSHIELD, a Re-
trieval-Augmented Generation framework for the DeFi transac-
tion attack detection. At a high level, TXSHIELD combines a
Historical Knowledge Base with a RAG-based Decision Engine
that retrieves cases, selects context, constructs prompts, and pro-
duces LLM judgments. For C1, we construct a knowledge base
by collecting execution traces from historical attack incidents,
extracting behavioral summaries using LLM-based semantic
summarization, and storing them as vector embeddings along-
side structured metadata. This transforms opaque execution
traces into interpretable attack patterns. For C2, we design a
two-stage retrieval mechanism: the first stage performs vector
similarity search to retrieve top-k candidate cases; the second
stage applies a similarity threshold (τ = 0.85) to filter out low-
confidence matches, retaining cases that meet the similarity
criterion. For C3, XGBoost estimates risk from numerical
transaction features before LLM inference. When retrieved
cases are available, this risk estimate determines whether they
are retained as context. The prompt also includes the attack
probability as an auxiliary signal, while the LLM makes the
final decision from transaction evidence. Transactions without
retained context are analyzed without historical examples.
We construct DeFiAttackBench, an evaluation set combining
364 attack transactions from DeFiHackLabs [17] with 364 be-
nign transactions sampled from the affected protocols. The sys-
tem achieves 92.93% F1, compared with 86.48% for XGBoost-
only, and reduces the number of false-positive transactions
from 60 to 7. The variant without XGBoost achieves 77.94%
F1. The backbone ablation examines detection performance
across nine LLMs. On a 91-attack subset of the D1 price-
manipulation dataset introduced by DeFiScope [13], which
we denote PriceAttackBench, the system detects 86 attacks
(94.51%). These experiments examine both general transaction
detection and specialized price-manipulation detection.
This paper makes the following contributions:
• We present TXSHIELD, the first RAG-based framework
for the DeFi transaction attack detection that leverages
historical attack knowledge to enhance LLM reasoning,
addressing the limitations of both rule-based and pure
LLM approaches.
• We introduce risk-guided context selection and prompting
to address the ambiguity between resemblance to historical
attacks and malicious intent. An XGBoost risk estimate
controls the use of retrieved cases and informs LLM
reasoning, while transaction evidence determines the final
classification.

a direct XGBoost baseline, component ablations, and
backbone ablations across nine LLMs. The combined
system improves F1 and reduces false positives relative
to direct numerical classification.
• We characterize the strengths and limitations of trace-
based semantic reasoning across attack categories, identi-
fying the behavioral evidence that supports detection and
the contextual requirements that limit it.

II. BACKGROUND
a) Smart Contracts and DeFi Ecosystem: Smart con-
tracts are self-executing programs deployed on blockchain
platforms such as Ethereum. Written primarily in Solidity,
these contracts are compiled into bytecode and executed by
the Ethereum Virtual Machine (EVM), a stack-based virtual
machine that processes transactions deterministically across
all network nodes. Once deployed, smart contract code is
immutable (it cannot be modified or patched), which makes se-
curity vulnerabilities particularly consequential. Decentralized
Finance (DeFi) leverages smart contracts to replicate traditional
financial services without centralized intermediaries [7]. The
DeFi ecosystem comprises several key protocol types: (1)
Decentralized Exchanges (DEXs) such as Uniswap [18] enable
permissionless token trading through Automated Market Mak-
ers (AMMs), where liquidity pools and mathematical formulas
(e.g., the constant-product formula x · y = k) determine token
prices; (2) Lending Protocols like Aave [19] and Compound
allow users to lend and borrow assets with interest rates
determined algorithmically; and (3) Flash Loan Providers offer
uncollateralized loans that must be borrowed and repaid within
a single atomic transaction [4]. A defining characteristic of
DeFi is composability (the ability for protocols to interact
seamlessly, with outputs from one protocol serving as inputs
to another). While composability enables capital efficiency

3
and innovation, it also creates complex attack surfaces where
vulnerabilities can span multiple protocols.
b) DeFi Attack Taxonomy: Based on existing litera-
ture [7], [20], [21], we categorize DeFi attacks into the
following major types:
Reentrancy Attacks. Reentrancy occurs when a contract
makes an external call to another contract before updating
its own state, allowing the callee to “re-enter” the original
function and exploit the inconsistent state [22]. Modern variants
include cross-function reentrancy (exploiting state shared across
multiple functions) and cross-contract reentrancy (exploiting
interactions between multiple contracts) [23], [24].
Price Manipulation Attacks. These attacks exploit the mech-
anism by which DeFi protocols determine asset prices. In
AMM-based DEXs, an attacker can execute large trades to
temporarily shift the price curve, then exploit this artificial price
in another protocol (e.g., a lending platform using the DEX as
a price oracle) [10], [25]. Flash loans amplify this threat by
providing attackers with massive capital at zero upfront cost [4].
Time-Weighted Average Price (TWAP) oracles, designed to
mitigate single-block manipulation, remain vulnerable to multi-
block attacks [26].
Access Control Vulnerabilities. These vulnerabilities arise
when privileged functions lack proper authorization checks, al-
lowing unauthorized users to execute administrative operations
such as minting tokens, modifying parameters, or draining
funds [15]. Variants include missing access modifiers, incorrect
role assignments, and unprotected initialization functions.
Business Logic Flaws. Unlike generic vulnerability pat-
terns, business logic flaws are protocol-specific bugs in the
implementation of intended functionality. Examples include
incorrect reward calculations, flawed liquidation mechanisms,
and improper handling of edge cases. These vulnerabilities
are particularly challenging to detect because they require
understanding the protocol’s intended behavior [16].
Other Vulnerabilities. Additional attack vectors include
integer overflow/underflow (largely mitigated by Solidity 0.8+),
front-running and sandwich attacks exploiting transaction or-
dering [27], and governance attacks manipulating decentralized
voting mechanisms.
c) Retrieval-Augmented
Generation:
Retrieval-
Augmented Generation (RAG) is a paradigm that enhances
LLM
capabilities
by
incorporating
external
knowledge
retrieval [28]. Unlike pure parametric models that rely solely
on knowledge encoded in their weights during training,
RAG systems dynamically retrieve relevant information from
external databases to augment the generation process. A
typical RAG architecture consists of three components: (1) a
knowledge base storing documents as vector embeddings, (2)
a retriever that identifies relevant documents given a query,
and (3) a generator (typically an LLM) that produces outputs
conditioned on both the query and retrieved context. The
retrieval process typically employs dense vector similarity
search, where queries and documents are mapped to a
shared embedding space and relevance is measured by cosine
similarity or other distance metrics. RAG supplements an
LLM's pretrained knowledge with information retrieved
from an external knowledge base. The retrieved information
provides context and evidence for the model's responses [28],
[29].
III. APPROACH OVERVIEW
This section presents the design of TXSHIELD, a retrieval-
augmented framework for transaction-level DeFi attack de-
tection. We first provide an overview of the system architec-
ture and then detail each component: the historical knowledge
base construction pipeline, retrieval with similarity filtering,
XGBoost-guided context selection, and risk-informed LLM
reasoning.
As illustrated in Figure 1, TXSHIELD combines a historical
knowledge base with a RAG-based decision engine. The engine
retrieves historical cases, uses XGBoost to select context and
supply a risk hint, and invokes the LLM for the final judgment.
The Historical Knowledge Base serves as an external memory
that stores structured representations of past attack incidents.
Unlike static rule sets, this knowledge base captures behavioral
semantics through high-level descriptions of how attacks unfold.
These descriptions enable TXSHIELD to recognize novel attack
variants that share similar patterns with known incidents.
The RAG-based Decision Engine preprocesses transaction
traces and retrieves historical cases using similarity search and
threshold filtering. When retrieval supplies context, XGBoost
estimates transaction risk from numerical trace features. Low-
risk scores cause the engine to discard the retrieved context
and switch to analysis without historical examples; otherwise,
the context is retained. The risk probability also informs the
prompt. The LLM then produces the final classification and
its supporting explanation.
This architecture addresses the three challenges identified in
Section I: the knowledge base construction bridges the semantic
gap (C1) by transforming low-level traces into interpretable
summaries; the two-stage retrieval addresses context selection
(C2) by filtering candidate cases; and the context-dependent
prompt selection enhances reasoning reliability (C3) by ground-
ing LLM decisions in relevant evidence.
IV. SECURITY KNOWLEDGE CONSTRUCTION
The knowledge base construction pipeline transforms raw
historical attack data into a searchable repository of behavioral
patterns. Construction consists of execution trace minimization,
semantic summarization, and vector indexing.
We source historical attack incidents from DeFiHack-
Labs [17], a curated repository containing Proof-of-Concept
(PoC) implementations of real-world DeFi exploits. The
historical knowledge base contains 209 entries. Each PoC
is a Foundry [30] test case that reproduces the attack on a
forked blockchain state. For each incident, we execute the PoC
and collect:
• Invocation Flow: The sequence of external and internal
function calls, including contract addresses, function
signatures, and call parameters.
• State Changes: Modifications to contract storage, particu-
larly balance changes for relevant tokens.
• Event Logs: Emitted events that signal significant opera-
tions (e.g., Transfer, Swap, Borrow).

The raw trace data captures the very execution details of an
attack but often lacks the semantic context needed for effective
analysis. To eliminate execution noise and reduce the trace size,
we apply a minimization algorithm that removes redundant or
low-information nodes while preserving the essential control-
flow and asset-flow semantics of the attack.
A. Execution Trace Minimization
Raw execution traces can exceed an LLM's context win-
dow and contain redundant details that hinder analysis. Our
investigation reveals that exploit transactions exhibit similar
execution patterns. First, most of them have manipulated
balance of contract accounts, through the operations like send
or transfer. Second, many of them involve complex contract
interactions through external calls, such as flashloan that
borrows a large amount of tokens from a lending protocol.
With these observations, we devise an algorithm to filter out
low-information content within raw execution traces while
preserving attack semantics.
As shown in Algorithm 1, the minimization process consists
of three stages: duplicate removal, general pruning, and target-
oriented pruning. First, we identify repeated call nodes through
a depth-first traversal (Lines 10–17). A call is represented by a
signature tuple ⟨type, contract, function, args, value, return⟩
indicating its call type, contract address, function name, argu-
ments, transferred value, and return value (Line 11). Within
each trace tree, only the first occurrence of a signature is
retained (Line 15); subsequent occurrences and their subtrees
are removed during pruning stage (Lines 19–20).
Second, we recursively apply general pruning and normal-
ization rules (Lines 18–34). Nodes beyond the maximum
traversal depth are replaced by truncation markers to preserve
the existence of deeper execution while bounding the trace
size (Line 22). We remove only low-information static metadata
queries, namely calls associated with decimals, symbol,
and name (Lines 23–24). In contrast, other state-dependent
queries such as balanceOf and getReserves, as well as
event nodes, are retained because they may expose asset-flow
or protocol-state evidence. Long argument strings are truncated
to a fixed length, and contract addresses are converted to
compact aliases while preserving their distinguishing prefixes
and suffixes (Lines 31–33).
Finally, we perform target-oriented structural pruning if target
function(s) are given. After locating the target function ft,
such as executeOperation, we record the depth of its
first occurrence (Line 6). At that depth, nodes that do not
invoke ft are removed, whereas nodes at other depths remain
subject only to the general pruning rules (Line 8, Lines 35–41).
This step suppresses parallel calls that are less relevant to
the monitored execution phase while retaining the context
leading to the target call and its nested internal behavior. The
resulting minimized tree reduces redundant and low-information
content while preserving control-flow, asset-flow, and state-
query evidence relevant to attack monitoring.
B. Semantic Summarization and Vector Indexing
Raw execution traces are verbose and difficult to interpret
directly. We use an LLM to generate concise behavioral
summaries that describe the attack logic in natural language.
The summarization prompt instructs the model to:
1) Identify the attack type (e.g., price manipulation, reen-
trancy, access control).
2) Describe the attack flow in 3–5 steps, focusing on the
sequence of operations and their effects.
3) Highlight key indicators: unusual fund movements, suspi-
cious call patterns, or state manipulation.

For example, a flash-loan price manipulation attack might be
summarized as: “The attacker borrows 10 M USDC via an Aave
flash loan, swaps to ETH on Uniswap to inflate the ETH/USDC
price, deposits ETH as collateral on the victim lending protocol
(which uses Uniswap as a price oracle), borrows the maximum
amount of USDC against the inflated collateral value, repays
the flash loan, and profits $2.3 M from the price discrepancy.”
These summaries provide human-readable documentation and,
more importantly, distill the semantic essence of attacks into a
concise form that offers more useful context for downstream
analysis and retrieval.
Each attack case is stored as a record containing: (1) a unique
case ID, (2) the behavioral summary, (3) the raw execution trace,
and (4) structured metadata. We generate vector embeddings
for the behavioral summaries using Jina Embeddings v3 [31]
and index them in a vector database. The vector representation
enables semantic similarity search: when a new transaction
exhibits patterns similar to known attacks, relevant historical
cases can be retrieved even if the specific contracts, function
names, or token addresses differ. This “semantic fingerprinting”
is the foundation of TXSHIELD’s generalization capability.

V. IN-CONTEXT REASONING FOR ATTACK DETECTION
Na¨ıve similarity search can retrieve cases that are superfi-
cially similar but semantically irrelevant, introducing noise that
degrades detection accuracy. We design a two-stage retrieval
mechanism to ensure high-precision context for LLM reasoning.
A. Knowledge Retrieval
Given an incoming transaction, TXSHIELD first preprocesses
it to extract the execution trace and generates a query rep-
resentation. This query is embedded using the same model
as the knowledge base, and we perform approximate nearest
neighbor search to retrieve the top-k most similar historical
cases. We compute pairwise similarity based on the L2 distance
in the embedding space: sim(q, d) = 1 −∥q −d∥2, where q is
the query embedding and d is a document embedding from
the knowledge base. Higher scores indicate greater semantic
similarity. We set k = 5 to balance recall and computational
efficiency.
Not all retrieved cases are equally relevant. To filter out
low-confidence matches, we apply a similarity threshold τ:
Cfiltered = {di ∈Top-k | sim(q, di) ≥τ}. We empirically set
τ = 0.85 based on validation experiments that examined the
correlation between similarity scores and detection accuracy.
Cases below this threshold are discarded to prevent the LLM
from being misled by tangentially related examples.
This two-stage mechanism serves as a “quality gate”: Stage 1
casts a wide net to capture potentially relevant cases, while
Stage 2 refines the results to retain only high-confidence
matches. If no cases satisfy the threshold (Cfiltered = ∅),
TXSHIELD proceeds without retrieval augmentation.
B. XGBoost-guided Retrieval and Prompting
Benign and malicious DeFi transactions can share flash
loans, large transfers, and multi-contract interactions. Retrieval
based on these similarities can introduce historical attacks that
resemble the current transaction without explaining its intent.
Such examples add noise to the prompt and may steer the
LLM toward an attack interpretation even when the transaction
is benign. The system therefore filters the retrieved context
before using it for analysis.
An XGBoost classifier trained on attack and benign trans-
actions estimates the current transaction’s attack probability
from numerical trace features. This provides a transaction-level
criterion for deciding whether to retain the retrieved context.
When retrieval returns eligible cases, the system retains them if
the estimated probability meets the context-selection threshold
and discards them otherwise. Without eligible cases, the LLM
analyzes the transaction without historical examples. This gate
is intended to reduce misleading attack context while preserving
the LLM’s ability to identify attacks from execution evidence.
When available, the probability is included in the prompt
as an ML correction hint. Because XGBoost is trained on a
balanced set of attack and benign transactions, we use 0.5
as the reference threshold for this hint. Below this threshold,
the hint asks the LLM to scrutinize potential false positives,
while requiring the final decision to be justified by transaction
evidence. XGBoost thus guides context selection and prompting,
while the LLM makes the final classification.
The classifier uses ten transaction features: call count, event
count, maximum call depth, unique contract count, indicators
for transfer, approve, and flashloan operations, total call-node
value, unique signature count, and unknown signature count.
We apply a logarithmic transformation to the total call-node
value to compress its numerical range, adding one before taking
the logarithm so that zero values remain valid. Call-node values
represent native transfers rather than normalized ERC-20 asset
flows.
C. Risk-informed LLM Reasoning
We use Kimi-k3 as the default LLM backbone for detection
on DeFiAttackBench and component ablations. The backbone
ablation replaces this model while retaining the rest of the
detection pipeline.
The LLM makes the final attack-versus-benign decision from
transaction evidence. Prompt selection depends on whether
historical context is retained. With historical cases, the prompt
asks the LLM to investigate the attack hypothesis suggested
by similar traces and distinguish benign look-alikes using ex-
ecution evidence. Without historical cases, the prompt starts
from a benign interpretation and requires evidence of mali-
cious behavior before reporting an attack. When available, the
XGBoost probability provides an additional cue to scrutinize
potential false positives. The LLM evaluates the suggested
attack mechanism against the current transaction evidence.
When historical cases remain after similarity filtering and
XGBoost-guided context selection, we construct an augmented
prompt that includes:
1) A role definition establishing the LLM as an expert in
smart contract attack detection.
2) The current transaction, i.e., the execution trace of the
transaction under analysis.
3) Historical context: behavioral summaries and metadata of
retrieved cases, with case IDs mapped to detailed security
descriptions.
4) An auxiliary risk hint containing the XGBoost attack
probability, when available.
5) Analysis instructions directing the LLM to compare the
current transaction against historical patterns, identify
shared malicious indicators, and assess the degree of
behavioral correspondence.

Datasets. We use a transaction dataset constructed for
this study and a price-manipulation dataset introduced by
DeFiScope [13].
DeFiAttackBench. We construct this evaluation set from 364
attack transactions drawn from DeFiHackLabs [17], a public
repository of real-world exploit reproductions, and 364 benign
transactions sampled from normal user activity in the affected
protocols. The benign transactions are selected using temporal
proximity to the attacks. We call the resulting balanced set
of 728 transactions DeFiAttackBench. The attack cases span
January 2021 to January 2025 and include 110+ labeled exploit
types across blockchains such as Ethereum, BSC, and Arbitrum.
We randomly shuffle the overall transaction collection before
partitioning it for model training, knowledge-base construction,
and evaluation. The 728 test transactions are disjoint from
both the transactions used to train XGBoost and those used
to construct the RAG knowledge base. Figure 4 shows the
attack distribution and their proportions. Business logic flaws
and price-related attacks are the most common, accounting
for 34.3% and 17.9% of the dataset, respectively. Reentrancy
and call attacks, access control vulnerabilities, input validation
issues, flashloan-driven attacks, mathematical calculation issues,
token mechanism vulnerabilities, and other miscellaneous attack
types make up the remaining 47.8%.
PriceAttackBench. We use the D1 dataset introduced by
DeFiScope [13] for price-manipulation detection. DeFiScope as-
sembled its 95 real-world attacks from DeFort, DeFiHackLabs,
and its industry partner, removing duplicate cases across these
sources. The attacks caused a reported total loss of $381.16M
on Ethereum and BSC. We could not parse transaction data for
four cases and evaluate the remaining 91 attack transactions.
We denote this evaluated subset PriceAttackBench throughout
the paper.

E. Discussion: Operational Boundaries and Capability Spec-
trum
Table VIII summarizes detection rates by attack category. We
interpret the differences through three properties: whether the
trace exposes discriminative behavior, whether the knowledge
base contains analogous attack patterns, and whether identifying
malicious intent requires context spanning multiple calls or
contracts. These properties help explain where transaction-
level reasoning succeeds and where evidence is fragmented or
missing.
We label each category in Table VIII by the primary bottle-
neck it presents to TXSHIELD. Low: high observability and
strong analogicality (clear, repetitive trace signatures). Medium:
observable core semantics but weaker reusability due to
protocol-specific or highly variable logic (analogy-dependent).
High: strong contextual dependency, where intent emerges only
after aggregating evidence across long, multi-step, or cross-
contract execution chains. Fundamental: low observability at
the trace-semantic level, where execution remains behaviorally
compliant and the malice stems from cryptographic or protocol-
assumption violations beyond behavior-only semantics.
TXSHIELD demonstrates its most resilient detection capabili-
ties in scenarios where behavioral anomalies are highly observ-
able and semantic patterns remain stable and reusable. Typical
examples include classic reentrancy exploits (96.2%), flash-loan
price manipulations (91.7%), and price/oracle manipulations
(93.2%). These attacks generate explicit, identifiable signatures
within the execution flow, such as the “unprivileged call to
privileged function” pattern seen in the LocalTrader series,
or the “unexpected state change following an external call”
characteristic of the SpankChain reentrancy exploit. Because the
core malicious semantics of these attacks are highly repetitive,

TXSHIELD can leverage its historical case library to establish

Consequently, even when encountering novel variants, the
system maintains high detection rates through stable pattern

For more sophisticated DeFi exploits, detection performance
varies with the complexity and novelty of the attack patterns.
These exploits include access control (79.3%), business logic
flaws (89.6%), arbitrary external calls (86.7%), liquidity/migra-
tion exploits (87.5%), reflection/token economics flaws, and
storage/proxy issues. In this domain, TXSHIELD effectively
identifies signatures such as the “unprivileged call to privileged
function” pattern (e.g., LocalTrader series) or anomalous asset
transfer sequences when the knowledge base contains analogous
patterns (e.g., Platypus attack). However, performance is
challenged by highly innovative or combinatorial variants. For
instance, the Indexed Finance exploit utilized a then-novel
index token weight rebalancing mechanism; the absence of
directly comparable cases in the knowledge base can lead to
false negatives. Similarly, variants employing non-standard
mechanisms require the underlying LLM to reason about
novel logic rather than rely on simple pattern matching. One
example is the UwU Lend exploit’s use of median pricing across
multiple pools. This area represents a transition for TXSHIELD
from “pattern matching” to “semantic comprehension,” where
accuracy is strictly gated by the breadth of the knowledge base
and the model’s reasoning depth.
A more pronounced performance degradation is observed in
attacks where malicious intent is decoupled from immediate
actions or dispersed across long, complex execution chains.
Cross-contract reentrancy (e.g., Rari Capital), signature and
validation issues (57.1%), and fake market/MEV/sandwich
attacks (75.0%) exemplify this challenge. The core difficulty
lies in the fragmentation of semantic signals: malicious intent
is often distributed across multiple contracts or embedded
within sequences of seemingly legitimate operations, creating a
bottleneck for contextual integration. Intent typically becomes
discernible only after aggregating evidence across disparate
calls, such as missing ecrecover, replay patterns, MEV
ordering, or long-range state effects. Although TXSHIELD’s
retrieval module can surface localized anomalous fragments,
its ability to reconstruct a holistic cross-contract call-graph
and synthesize these dispersed signals into a coherent attack
narrative remains limited, resulting in lower detection rates
(approximately 57%–75%).
Cryptographic exploits have detection rates below 50% in
the category-level evaluation. Attacks involving signature mal-
leability or predictable randomness require analysis of the
corresponding security assumptions, which may not be explicit
in a transaction trace. Numerical errors, such as overflow or
precision loss, can similarly require reasoning about expected
arithmetic behavior. Combining trace analysis with checks of
these properties is a direction for extending detection coverage.
Individual incidents also illustrate the diversity of failure
mechanisms. DeFiHackLabs documents the February 2024
FILX DN404 incident as an access-control exploit [36]. The
Game exploit combines a bidding-logic flaw with reentrant
calls
[37], illustrating why analysis may need to connect
multiple interacting behaviors within one trace.
The category-level results indicate that detection is strongest
when execution traces expose recognizable attack behavior.
Fragmented cross-contract evidence and attacks without clear
behavioral signatures remain difficult. These findings motivate
richer call-graph and state-flow representations and comple-
mentary analyses for properties that transaction traces alone
do not establish.
VII. RELATED WORK
On-chain Security and Program Analysis. The im-
mutability of deployed smart contracts has driven extensive
research on on-chain security analysis, broadly encompassing
pre-deployment verification and post-deployment transaction
monitoring. Pre-deployment approaches rely on static analysis
and formal verification, with symbolic execution tools such
as Oyente [38] and Maian [39], and mature frameworks like
Slither [5] and Mythril [6], enabling automated detection of
common vulnerabilities. Formal methods, including Certora
Prover [8] and KEVM [9], further strengthen correctness
guarantees by reasoning over mathematically precise semantics.
However, static analysis is fundamentally constrained by path
explosion and its inability to capture the non-deterministic,
evolving global state of blockchain systems, particularly in
DeFi environments characterized by composability [7] and gas-
related vulnerabilities [40]. These limitations have motivated
post-deployment transaction analysis, where graph-based meth-
ods, machine learning techniques [41], and forensic tools such
as TXSPECTOR [32] are used to identify malicious behaviors
including Ponzi schemes and flash-loan-based attacks [27].
Nevertheless, existing dynamic approaches largely depend on
predefined heuristics, leaving open the challenge of detecting
malicious interactions that remain syntactically compliant yet
exploit subtle business logic manipulations.
LLMs for Code and Security Analysis. The emergence of
Large Language Models (LLMs) has initiated a paradigm shift
in smart contract auditing, steering the field away from rigid
pattern matching toward nuanced semantic inference. Early
breakthroughs were rooted in representation learning, where

PAGE 13
13
models like CodeBERT [42] and SmartBERT demonstrated
a profound capacity for capturing program control flows and
semantics through domain-specific pre-training. Building upon
this foundation, the focus has shifted toward fine-grained defect
localization, with models such as VulBERTa [43] and LineVul
[44] significantly enhancing the precision of vulnerability
prediction.
More recently, LLM-driven frameworks like GPTScan [15]
have begun integrating reasoning capabilities with program
analysis to validate potential attack vectors. Chain-of-Thought
(CoT) prompting [45] is often used to enhance these reasoning
capabilities. Nevertheless, deploying LLMs in the high-stakes
DeFi environment is not without its perils. The most pervasive
challenge is the phenomenon of “hallucinations”, wherein mod-
els generate plausible yet technically erroneous vulnerability
reports [46]. Furthermore, the inherent “knowledge cutoff” of
pre-trained models renders them blind to zero-day exploits and
rapidly evolving protocol patterns. This lack of interpretability,
often referred to as the “black-box” nature of LLMs [47],
and their performance degradation when operating outside
their “comfort zone” [48], further complicates their adoption
by security professionals who require verifiable evidence for
decision-making.
RAG in Software Engineering and Security. Retrieval-aug-
mented generation conditions LLM responses on external evi-
dence [28]. In security analysis, this can provide task-specific
context beyond the model's parametric knowledge.
Retrieval and LLM-based assistance have been studied in
repository-level code completion (RepoCoder [49]), documen-
tation-guided generation (DocPrompting [50]), and penetration
testing (PentestGPT [51]). SmartInv [52] studies invariant
inference for smart contracts. Our focus is transaction-level
attack detection, where relevant context includes execution
behavior and documented exploit mechanisms.
TXSHIELD retrieves behavioral summaries and execution
traces from historical attacks to support transaction-level de-
tection. It combines this context with numerical risk guidance
and asks the LLM to assess the current transaction against the
retrieved evidence. This connects historical attack knowledge
with the behavior observed in an individual execution trace.
VIII. CONCLUSION
We presented TXSHIELD, a retrieval-augmented framework
for transaction-level DeFi attack detection. Historical attack
summaries provide behavioral context, while XGBoost esti-
mates transaction risk to select that context and guide LLM
prompting. The LLM combines these inputs with execution
evidence to produce the final classification.
TXSHIELD achieves 92.93% F1 on DeFiAttackBench, the
728-transaction evaluation set constructed from DeFiHackLabs
attacks and sampled benign transactions. Compared with XG-
Boost-only, it improves F1 by 6.45 percentage points and
reduces the number of false-positive transactions from 60 to 7
at comparable recall. In the complementary evaluation on 91
attacks from DeFiScope's D1 dataset [13], it detects 86 attacks.
These results support combining numerical risk estimates with
historical context and transaction-level reasoning.
TXSHIELD faces limitations in scenarios involving cryp-
tographic exploits without behavioral signatures, complex
multi-step cross-contract attacks with fragmented semantic
signals, and truly novel attack patterns that are absent from the
knowledge base. Future work includes integrating formal veri-
fication for cryptographic exploit detection, developing more
sophisticated call-graph analysis for cross-contract reasoning,
and implementing automated knowledge base expansion to
remain responsive to emerging threats. The results show the
value of using historical attack evidence to support transaction
analysis, with numerical risk guidance controlling how that
evidence enters LLM reasoning.
